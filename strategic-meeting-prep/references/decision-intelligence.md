# Decision intelligence: what can the meeting legitimately decide?

Use for board/executive approval, supplier escalation, strategy, investment, project scope, legal/compliance, and any choice with meaningful downside.

## Decision stack (answer in sequence)

1. **Decision statement**: who can authorize what change, by when, and for which accountable scope? If the decision owner is unknown, this is a discovery meeting.
2. **Evidence standard**: which facts and acceptance thresholds are prerequisite? Mark observed fact, reported claim, inference, unknown, and as-of date.
3. **Constraints**: law, safety, budget, commitments, dependencies, reversible vs irreversible, stakeholder impact, time-to-decision.
4. **Options**: usually choose approve, conditional approve / pilot, defer and verify, reject; keep options plausible and nonduplicative.
5. **Recommendation**: only when evidence supports it. Otherwise recommend the next *information-generating* action.
6. **Review trigger**: what result, document, or deadline changes the decision?

No invented numerical assumptions; any arithmetic should identify source and unit. Separate explicit costs from qualitative tradeoffs. When evidence is too weak, do not fabricate expected value or probabilities.

## Four traps

- **False binary**: "full approval now or project failure" hides staged pilots and discovery. Ask if decision can be decomposed.
- **Authority confusion**: an attendee may advise but not sign. Obtain signatory, governance and decision rights.
- **Sunk-cost pressure**: prior investment is not itself a reason to approve future spend; assess incremental effects.
- **Unvalidated precision**: forecasts, ROI or engineering targets become dangerous when quoted without methodology, observation period, units, and sensitivity.

## Pre-mortem and dissent

Write the most plausible failure story from the skeptical stakeholder's perspective, not the user's. Find at least one disconfirming observation that would reverse the recommended action. Keep dissent in the record; do not flatten into consensus. Explicitly distinguish hypothetical concerns from voiced objections.

## A 30-minute decision agenda example

- 0–3: objective, what can be decided, rights
- 3–9: evidence / change since last update; unresolved conflicts
- 9–17: 2–3 options and tradeoffs
- 17–24: objections, risks, decision threshold
- 24–28: decision or bounded verification mandate
- 28–30: recap exact owner, date, criteria and follow-up

Shortened meeting: 1-minute ask, 3-minute decisive evidence, 3-minute challenge, 3-minute decision or verification. State whether these timings are *proposed*, not confirmed.

## Worked case: evidence conflict

Marketing claims a 20% improvement in a draft; finance's signed report shows 8% for a different period. The right next step is not "choose the newest number". Extract period, denominator, scope and source authority. Put both figures in an unresolved evidence row, ask finance and marketing to reconcile the definitions, and offer a conditional sign-off on a measurement plan. A board should not endorse a quantified result from mixed denominators.
